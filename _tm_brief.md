## 5. §2(e)(1) Descriptiveness Analysis

**Issue:** Could USPTO examiner refuse BISTRO STEWARD under §2(e)(1) as merely descriptive of restaurant SaaS?

**Two-part term analysis:**

**"BISTRO"** — Dictionary noun for a small, casual European-style restaurant or café. As applied to restaurant SaaS, BISTRO describes the *buyer's type of business*, not the *software's features or function*. Relevant case law holds that marks describing the *purchaser's industry* (rather than describing the goods themselves) are at most suggestive, not merely descriptive. Compare: RESTAURANT TECHNOLOGY would describe the goods; BISTRO describes the consumer vertical. BISTRO alone does not immediately convey a significant ingredient, quality, or characteristic of inventory-management software.

**"STEWARD"** — Dictionary noun with two relevant meanings: (1) a person who manages another's property, finances, or affairs; (2) a hospitality staff role (flight steward, hotel steward). As applied to restaurant management SaaS, STEWARD could arguably suggest "management" or "oversight" — but does not *directly describe* what the software does without a multi-step mental inferential process. An examiner would need to reason: steward → manager → software manages things → software is a management tool. That chain of inference satisfies the test for suggestiveness, not mere descriptiveness.

**Compound analysis:** BISTRO + STEWARD together create a unitary impression: "the steward of the bistro" or "a restaurant's manager/guardian." This is a *suggestive* connotation — it implies benefit (having a reliable guardian for your restaurant operations) without *directly describing* inventory management, recipe costing, or SaaS delivery. No consumer seeing BISTRO STEWARD would immediately understand it to mean "downloadable software for restaurant inventory." Multiple inferential steps required.

**Analogous granted marks supporting suggestiveness:** The USPTO has registered numerous two-word marks combining industry descriptors + role/function words for SaaS products in restaurant/hospitality tech without §2(e)(1) refusals, including RESTAURANT365 (registered; restaurant accounting SaaS), MARGINEDGE (registered; restaurant P&L SaaS), TOUCHBISTRO (if live; POS SaaS). These demonstrate examiner tolerance for suggestive compound marks in the restaurant tech space.

**Conclusion:** §2(e)(1) refusal is unlikely but not impossible. If issued, response should argue: (a) BISTRO describes buyer's industry not goods; (b) STEWARD requires inferential leap; (c) compound creates suggestive unitary meaning; (d) analogous registered marks in restaurant SaaS space. Attorney should draft goods/services ID language to minimize descriptiveness exposure.
